title: 'Boku no Natsuyasumi 2: Umi no Bouken-hen 4/5'
pubDate: '2026-09-31'
---

Boku no Natsuyasumi 2 is, just like what came before (and after), a game about Boku, a city kid in Japan's 1970s, having to spend the month of August at his relatives' in the countryside due to his mother giving birth that month. But, perhaps confusingly for some people, it does so through the lens of his adult self looking back at said summer vacation. It's not a game about looking back with nostalgia at life as a kid and how much easier and better everything was back then; instead, it's a very nuanced one: the last summer as an only child, the last one by himself as a kid, without his parents around and without the self-imposed responsability that comes with being the big brother.

Since it's Boku's first time at the island, he doesn't know anyone than his family, and the place is completely new, so his experience translates well into ours as a player, with every discovery being new, every conversation, special. Still, with a more talkative Boku, one that takes part on other people's lives, that seems interested in helping others as much as possible, most of the time with a naive, childish approach to their problems, one that not only is appropiete for a kid but also feels sincere and comes from good will. How astonishingly simple it is to bring two old friends together: you, as Boku, arrange a meeting, make them talk to each other and things are back to normal! How about the old man who always wanted to have a grandson? Boku can be him. The greater the problem, the easier the solution.

I don't like to think or talk about sequels purely as evolutions, iterations of a formula, but rather as conversations and different points of view of the same ideas. While one could say that Boku no Natsuyasumi 2 expands on the ideas of the first game, with a greater area to explore, a bigger cast of characters and a wider range of subplots that take longer to resolve, in reality the experience is quite different. As close as the proposal seems at first, following the same daily structure and world design philosophy, the differences become aparent after just a few days. This is a more narrative-driven experience, one that is meant to be played in a certain way, following a schedule of "mandatory" events that are also longer and more interesting throughout. What was first a bunch of mostly unimportant setpieces, an arrangement of seemingly unimportant anecdotes in Boku's stay in the village, ones that you could miss and the game would still work, transforms into heavily narrative game that for some reason shares the open structure of the original, perhaps for lack of a better approach or just the unability to change the formula if they wanted their work funded. It's that perceived, inherited freedom that clashes with a game that needs a story to be told in order to exist.
